# Monolift in the research landscape: a deep survey

## Section 1. Introduction and Monolift positioning

**Monolift is a conservative bet on a radical idea.** Goodwin et al.'s PLOS '25 system distributes an annotated Go monolith onto Kubernetes as a compiler pass, without introducing a new language, runtime, or framework. The bet is that the last twenty years of "distributed from day one" cloud-native orthodoxy has over-paid for scalability in development velocity, and that a compiler can recover most of the scalability by reinterpreting a handful of annotated functions — *lifts* — as dual-dispatched local calls or remote microservice invocations. Coupled to each lift is a *delegate expression*, a small DSL that binds the placement decision to live performance signals. The program remains a runnable monolith when compiled without Monolift; this is the system's strongest developer-experience claim.

Four fault lines frame the rest of this document. **Automation versus annotation** asks how much of the lift boundary can be inferred rather than declared. **State, concurrency, and correctness** asks when the "no shared heap, no migration" bound holds and what the principled escape hatches are. **Placement as a decision problem** — composing delegate expressions into a stable global policy — is the deepest and least-solved of the four; the paper describes the per-lift machinery but does not characterize the global transition function, and the literature suggests this is where most of the engineering risk lives. **Beyond Go and Kubernetes** asks whether the abstraction survives a language change (WebAssembly, JVM, Rust) or a platform change (Lambda, Cloud Run, unikernel, edge).

The survey is organized to mirror those fault lines. Sections 2–4 take on the actor-model and placement foundations, which are where Monolift must defend novelty. Section 5 covers the offloading cost model that governs when a lift is worth the round trip. Sections 6–9 situate Monolift against multitier programming, choreographies, correctness mechanisms, and compiler infrastructure. Sections 10–14 cover evaluation, adjacent cloud-native frameworks, developer-experience questions, security, and platform generalization. Section 15 surfaces adjacent areas that belong in the conversation even though the brief did not mention them. Section 16 consolidates a concrete research agenda, and Section 17 collects references.

The thesis of the survey is that Monolift's genuine novelty is not the lift abstraction in isolation — variants exist in Service Weaver, Ignis, Offload Annotations, and Erlang — but the **conjunction of four properties**: compile-time insertion into unmodified Go, a monotonic fall-back semantics where an un-lifted build still runs, a per-lift runtime-directed policy language, and Kubernetes-as-backend with no bespoke runtime. Each property has prior art; the combination does not.

## Section 2. Distributed actors and the ghost of location transparency

### The argument that will not die

Any compiler that turns a local call into a remote one has to answer Waldo, Wyant, Wollrath, and Kendall's 1994 Sun technical report, *A Note on Distributed Computing* (TR-94-29). The note enumerates four irreducible differences between local and remote invocation — **latency, memory access semantics, partial failure, and concurrency** — and argues that any system that tries to paper over them will eventually force the programmer to confront exactly those differences at the worst possible time. The paper is the single most cited cautionary anchor in this area and its argument has aged extraordinarily well: CORBA, DCOM, Java RMI, and .NET Remoting all declared location transparency and all eventually reintroduced explicit distinctions (CORBA's `oneway` modifier, RMI's `RemoteException`, DCOM's apartment model). Michi Henning's *Rise and Fall of CORBA* (ACM Queue, 2006) gives the postmortem: the problem was not that CORBA hid distribution badly but that it hid it *aspirationally*, inviting developers into abstractions whose failure modes were worse than the problems they replaced.

**Monolift's answer is structural rather than rhetorical.** It does not claim transparency; it claims *opt-in per lift*. A function without an annotation never becomes a remote call. A function with an annotation acquires two dispatch paths, and the delegate expression selects between them based on live signals — failure, latency, saturation — that are precisely Waldo's four differences, promoted from invisible pitfalls to first-class inputs of the placement policy. In effect, Monolift concedes Waldo's critique and then treats the conceded properties as the API. This is a defensible position but it is also a demanding one: the delegate expression DSL is the surface where the four hard distributed-systems problems resurface, and any weakness in its semantics undoes the concession.

### Actors and the location-transparency lineage

The actor model (Hewitt, Bishop, Steiger, *IJCAI 1973*) is the oldest coherent answer to "how do I write a program where some calls go across the network?" Actors communicate only by asynchronous message passing and own private state; there is no shared memory to leak across a process boundary. **Erlang** (Armstrong, 1986; BEAM VM, 1998) is the industrial proof that the model scales, with lightweight processes, supervision trees, and hot code upgrade. **Akka** and **Akka Typed** (Lightbend, 2009–) bring the model to the JVM, adding explicit typed protocols after a decade of experience with untyped `ActorRef` mailboxes taught the community that dynamic typing at message boundaries is exactly the Waldo problem in a different accent.

**Orleans** (Bykov et al., *SoCC 2011*; Bernstein et al.) contributes the *virtual actor* or *grain*: an actor identified by a stable logical key that is materialized on some server on demand, with turn-based single-threaded execution and optional reentrancy. Orleans answers the "where does the actor live?" question with "don't ask; ask what it is." This is close in spirit to Monolift's runtime-directed placement: a lift is a piece of code whose location is a runtime property, not a source-level property. The difference is granularity — Orleans grains are long-lived stateful entities, Monolift lifts are stateless call-site reinterpretations — and consequently the kinds of failure Monolift has to model are simpler.

**Ray** (Moritz et al., *OSDI 2018*; Wang et al., *OSDI 2021*) is the most successful modern actor-adjacent system. Its `@ray.remote` decorator converts a Python function or class into a remotely-invokable task or actor; the Plasma object store holds argument and result values so that data movement is separable from task dispatch; the ownership model tracks the relationship between a task's caller (owner) and the produced object, enabling fate-sharing and lineage-based recovery. Ray is the closest existing analog to Monolift's annotation-plus-runtime model, and its ownership discipline — every remote object has exactly one owner, failures of the owner propagate — is a design pattern Monolift should take seriously when a lift's result outlives the caller's scope.

**Pony** (Clebsch, Drossopoulou, Blessing, McNeil, *AGERE! 2015*) is the formal high-water mark. Its *deny capabilities* — `iso`, `val`, `ref`, `box`, `tag`, `trn` — statically rule out data races without a garbage collector synchronization barrier. An `iso` reference is the only mutable reference to its object and can be sent between actors; a `val` is immutable and freely shareable. The capability lattice *is* the condition under which "no shared state" holds. Monolift targets Go, which has no analogous discipline, so the compiler must reconstruct Pony-style sendability via escape analysis and refuse lifts whose arguments or captures fail the test. **Cloud Haskell** (Epstein, Black, Peyton Jones, *Haskell Symp. 2011*) solved the same problem with static closures and serializable `Closure a` values; the lesson is the same, that crossing a process boundary is a typed operation.

**Emerald** (Jul, Levy, Hutchinson, Black, *TOCS 1988*) is an instructive failure: objects could migrate at runtime with `move` and `fix`, references were transparently updated, and yet developers found that reasoning about performance required knowing, at all times, where their objects actually lived. Emerald's transparency did not reduce cognitive load; it relocated it.

### Service Weaver: the framework with which Monolift must be compared

The most important contemporary comparison is **Service Weaver** (Ghemawat, Grandl, Petrovic, Whittaker, Patel, Shah, Voelker, Vahdat, *HotOS 2023*, "Towards Modern Development of Cloud Applications"). Service Weaver is Google's Go framework for writing distributed applications as a single modular binary in which components are Go interfaces annotated with `weaver.Implements[T]`; a code generator produces local stubs for co-located components and RPC clients/servers for components placed on different machines. The runtime topology is a deployment decision, not a source-code decision. In the paper's evaluation, an 11-microservice Go implementation at 10K QPS used 78 cores while the Service Weaver version used 28 cores, and colocating all 11 components in one OS process dropped that to 9 cores.

**This is the system Monolift competes with.** Martin Kleppmann's widely-quoted reaction on Twitter — *"Google's Service Weaver claims that it can hide the difference between RPC and local method calls. Just like CORBA claimed in 1991. Is it different this time?"* — is exactly the Waldo critique, and the HotOS paper does not have a satisfying answer to it. The differences between Service Weaver and Monolift are subtle but decisive. Service Weaver requires adoption of its component model: existing Go code must be rewritten as `weaver.Implements` interfaces with `weaver.Ref` dependencies. Monolift, by contrast, operates on unmodified Go with comment annotations, and the annotated program still runs as a monolith under the stock Go toolchain. Service Weaver's placement is fixed at deployment time in a TOML config; Monolift's placement is runtime-directed by delegate expressions. Service Weaver has a single notion of "component"; Monolift distinguishes the lift (a call-site transformation) from the deployment unit (a Kubernetes pod), letting the compiler amortize multiple lifts into one deployment. The two systems are converging on the same space from different starting points — Service Weaver from "write a monolith with a component DSL," Monolift from "annotate the monolith you already have" — and a head-to-head evaluation on DeathStarBench-scale workloads would probably be the most defensible empirical contribution the Monolift team could make.

**Dapr** (Microsoft, 2019–) occupies a different point: it is a sidecar runtime that exposes distributed-systems primitives (state stores, pub/sub, actors, secrets) over gRPC or HTTP to an application that remains language-agnostic. Dapr's actors are Orleans-derived virtual actors. The comparison with Monolift is that Dapr imposes zero compile-time work but substantial runtime cost (every call goes through the sidecar); Monolift inverts the tradeoff.

## Section 3. Automated partitioning and the annotation burden

### The automation–annotation spectrum

The deepest prior-art question Monolift must answer is whether its annotations are necessary. There is a thirty-year literature on systems that partition programs automatically, and Monolift sits toward the "annotation-heavy, automation-light" end of that spectrum by choice.

**Coign** (Hunt and Scott, *OSDI 1999*) is the founding example. It profiles a running COM application, builds a weighted graph of inter-component calls, and solves a graph-cut problem to place each component on the client or server. The insight is that the right partition is discoverable from runtime behavior; the limitation is that it works only because COM has explicit, coarse-grained component boundaries, and the optimum changes with workload in ways the one-shot solver never re-solves. **Pyxis** (Cheung, Arden, Madden, Myers, *VLDB 2012*) extends the idea to Java-plus-SQL: it statically partitions an application between the app server and the database to minimize round trips, using information-flow types to guarantee semantic equivalence. **CloneCloud** (Chun, Ihm, Maniatis, Naik, Patti, *EuroSys 2011*) and **MAUI** (Cuervo, Balasubramanian, Cho, Wolman, Saroiu, Chandra, Bahl, *MobiSys 2010*) solved the mobile-offload variant: MAUI with method-level annotations (`[Remoteable]`) and an ILP solver, CloneCloud with a VM-level thread migration. Both reported order-of-magnitude gains on compute-heavy workloads but *negative* gains on interactive or I/O-bound workloads, and both found that the profitable offload set shifts with network conditions. This is the earliest empirical statement of the result Monolift must live with: **any statically-chosen lift boundary is wrong a measurable fraction of the time.**

**Swift** (Chong, Liu, Myers, Qi, Vikram, Zheng, Zheng, *SOSP 2007*) is the cautionary analog on the security side. Swift partitions a web application between client and server using Jif's information-flow labels, placing each operation where its label permits and inserting the minimum replication necessary for correctness. It is beautiful but requires the entire program to be labeled — the annotation burden that Monolift explicitly refuses to impose. The lesson is not that labels are wrong but that the *granularity* of labels matters: Swift-style per-value labels are too expensive for a monolith retrofit; Monolift's per-function lift is probably close to the right granularity.

### Ignis: the paper Monolift must cite and distinguish itself from

**Ignis** (Vasilakis, Karel, Palkhiwala, Sonchack, DeHon, Smith, *PLDI 2019*, "Scaling Distribution-Oblivious Systems with Light-Touch Distribution") is the closest existing system to Monolift in framing. Ignis converts a legacy module-structured program (demonstrated on Node.js and Lua) into a distributed one by dynamically rewriting `import` to interpose profiling and RPC transformations at module boundaries. Two components do the work: *transformations*, which automatically wrap modules, record per-function statistics, and swap in RPC stubs when a module is promoted to a remote replica; and *recipes*, "lightweight annotations that guide the intended semantics of the resulting distribution," in the paper's own words. The system scales out only saturated components, under runtime control, with small and backward-compatible code changes.

The overlap with Monolift is extensive and must be discussed head-on. *Distribution-oblivious* maps onto Monolift's "annotated program still runs as a monolith." *Light-touch* maps onto comment-annotations. *Recipes* map onto delegate expressions. The differences Monolift can defensibly claim are that (i) Ignis operates at runtime via `import` interposition in dynamic languages, while Monolift operates at compile time on a statically-typed language, which enables stronger guarantees about what can safely cross a boundary; (ii) Ignis's recipes are per-module policies for scaling and replication, while delegate expressions are per-lift decisions that can flip bi-directionally on live signals; and (iii) Ignis treats distribution as a bottleneck-response mechanism, while Monolift frames it as an architectural choice with a dual-dispatch runtime. All three differences are real but narrow. **If the Monolift paper does not explicitly distinguish itself from Ignis, reviewers will.**

### The 2020–2025 ML/LLM wave in monolith decomposition

The automation literature has had a second life since 2020 driven by graph neural networks and LLMs. **Mono2Micro** (Kalia, Xiao, Krishna, Sinha, Vukovic, Banerjee, *FSE 2021*, IBM Research) combines runtime call traces with hierarchical clustering to propose microservice partitions for Java monoliths, and is the most cited industrial baseline. It was followed by **CARGO** (Nitin, Asthana, Ray, Krishna, *ASE 2022*), which uses AI-guided dependency analysis; **MAGNET** (Trabelsi, Moha, Guéhéneuc, Geffard, *ICSA 2024*), a GNN method for microservice identification; and **MonoEmbed** (Sellami and Saied, *arXiv 2502.04604*, Feb 2025), which uses contrastive learning and LoRA fine-tuning of LLMs to produce embeddings of monolithic components that are then clustered into microservices. A 2025 VAE-plus-GNN case study (Sooksatra, Maharjan, Černý, *Future Internet*, 2025) benchmarks these techniques on Java applications. Abgaz et al.'s *IEEE TSE 2023* systematic review ("Decomposition of Monolith Applications into Microservices Architectures") and the 2025 *Information and Software Technology* mapping study by Martínez Saucedo et al. are the two best entry points to this literature.

The honest assessment for Monolift: these systems do *offline, one-shot* decomposition — they propose a partition, a human approves it, and a rewrite happens. **None of them do what Monolift does**, which is keep a single source tree with annotations that can be toggled, and make placement decisions at runtime. An interesting research extension is to use an LLM-based decomposer as an *annotation suggester* for Monolift: given an unmarked monolith, propose a lift annotation set with justifications. This is complementary to Monolift, not competitive, and is probably the right 2026 workshop paper.

### The annotation burden problem

When does annotation become more expensive than rewriting? The empirical literature here is thinner than it should be, but the shape is consistent across OpenMP, gradual typing, and information-flow systems. **OpenMP** adoption studies (Lee and Eigenmann's work on OpenMP auto-parallelization, plus community surveys) report that initial parallelization is typically 5–15 lines of `#pragma omp` per thousand lines of C/C++, but *correct* parallelization with reduction clauses, data-sharing attributes, and SIMD hints doubles or triples that. **Gradual typing** studies on Python (Khan et al. on mypy adoption; the TypeScript migration reports from Airbnb and Dropbox) find that the incremental cost of adding type annotations is sub-linear in code size when the types are inferrable and super-linear when they require refactoring for type-safety. **Jif** (Myers and Liskov, *TOCS 2000*) information-flow annotations have been repeatedly reported by successor systems as the reason Jif was not adopted in practice.

The pattern is that annotation burden is acceptable when annotations are **local, optional, and monotonic** — you can add one annotation and see its effect without cascading changes. Monolift's lift annotations satisfy all three criteria by design: a lift is local to a function, optional in that the program still compiles without them, and monotonic in that adding a lift does not force rewrites elsewhere. The delegate expression sub-DSL is where this property is most at risk; if delegate expressions acquire dependencies on each other, the annotations become non-local and the monolith-rewrite cost returns.

## Section 4. Placement, scheduling, and the transition function

This is the section the Monolift research agenda most needs, because the per-lift policy is the published artifact but the global composition of delegate expressions is the unpublished one. The literature suggests that the global composition problem is where engineering energy should go.

### 4a. Application-level autoscaling

The research lineage runs from control-theoretic adaptive systems to learning-augmented schedulers. **SEEC** (Hoffmann, Maggio, Santambrogio, Leva, Agarwal, *ASPLOS 2011*) proposed a self-aware computing substrate where applications declare goals (performance, power) and a control loop adjusts resources. **POET** (Imes, Kim, Pal, Hoffmann, *2015*) extended it to energy-constrained embedded systems with PID-controlled frequency and core allocation. These systems established the vocabulary: a *sensor* produces a signal, a *policy* (controller) produces an action, *stability* is a proof obligation on the composition. Monolift's delegate expressions are policies in this sense, and the relevant question is whether a collection of delegate expressions admits any stability argument.

**Autopilot** (Rzadca, Findeisen, Świderski, Zych, Jagiełło, Henkel, Beyer, Dulloor, Burnett, Bogunovic, Brook, Chesterfield, Hand, Iwański, Kowalczyk, Petranović, Ponomarenko, Rockl, Schott, Stumpe, Vikram, Wayne, Wilkes, *EuroSys 2020*) is Google's production autoscaler. It combines a moving-window histogram of resource usage with a set of recommender rules and an ML model that proposes CPU/memory limits; the paper reports that ML recommendations reduce slack by 23% versus the human baseline while halving the rate of out-of-memory kills. The key lesson for Monolift is that production autoscaling is **a hybrid of reactive control and predictive ML**, not either one alone, and that operator trust — manifested in the histogram baseline — is what keeps the ML recommender honest.

**FIRM** (Qiu, Banerjee, Jha, Kalbarczyk, Iyer, *OSDI 2020*) applies two-level reinforcement learning to microservice resource management: an SVM identifies the critical path whose services cause SLO violations, and a DDPG agent re-allocates resources to those services. **Cilantro** (Bhardwaj, Kim, Pavuluri, Tumanov, *OSDI 2023*) reframes the problem as resource allocation with performance feedback, using a Thompson-sampling bandit to select allocations and explicitly handling the exploration–exploitation tradeoff. Cilantro is particularly instructive for Monolift because its feedback loop is *per-tenant* and its composition across tenants is proven to converge under mild assumptions; this is the kind of proof that delegate-expression composition lacks.

Kubernetes HPA, VPA, and KEDA are the baselines Monolift will be compared against. The HPA algorithm is a simple ratio rule: `desiredReplicas = ceil(currentReplicas * currentMetric / targetMetric)`, evaluated every 15s, with a 5-minute downscale stabilization window to suppress oscillation. Google's GKE documentation and multiple practitioner reports confirm that thrashing is the dominant failure mode, and that mitigations are all variants of hysteresis: cooldown periods, stabilization windows, and two-threshold scale-up/scale-down rules. **Monolift inherits this problem multiplied by the number of delegate expressions.**

### 4b. Control theory and the stability obligation

The control-theory toolkit Monolift needs is mostly classical. A **PID controller** on per-lift latency is the obvious starting point: the signal is a windowed p99, the setpoint is the SLO, the actuator is the local-versus-remote bit. A PID controller with a latency setpoint and a binary actuator degenerates to a Schmitt trigger — a **two-threshold hysteresis** element — and the design question is the gap between the thresholds. **SEDA** (Welsh, Culler, Brewer, *SOSP 2001*) gives the cleanest pedagogy: per-stage event queues with admission control based on queue length, and dynamic resource controllers that scale threads per stage. The SEDA result that matters for Monolift is that *per-stage controllers compose well as long as the stages share no global resource knob*; once they do, policy interactions produce oscillation. This is exactly the warning for delegate expressions that share CPU and network on the same node.

**Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty** techniques from Neely's queueing-theoretic optimization (*2010*) are the formal tool for proving that a greedy per-queue policy achieves throughput-optimal stability. In principle Monolift could recast its placement problem as a Lyapunov optimization over per-lift queues, and a workshop paper on "a Lyapunov formulation of delegate expressions" is a natural 2026 direction.

On the learning side, **CherryPick** (Alipourfard, Liu, Chen, Venkataraman, Yu, Zhang, *NSDI 2017*) uses Bayesian optimization to pick cloud VM types for recurring jobs with few trial runs; it is directly applicable to *offline* tuning of delegate-expression thresholds. Multi-armed bandits (UCB, Thompson sampling) are the online analog and have been used in CherryPick's successors.

### 4c. Microservice placement and latency awareness

Dean and Barroso's **"The Tail at Scale"** (*CACM 2013*) is the foundational article: when a user request fans out to N services, the user-observed latency is the tail of the slowest one, so even 1-in-1000 slow responses dominate at fan-out of 100. The implication for Monolift is that a delegate expression that optimizes mean latency is wrong; it must target a percentile, typically p99 or p99.9, and must account for *fan-out amplification* when a single request triggers multiple lift dispatches.

**DeathStarBench** (Gan, Zhang, Cheng, Shetty, Rathi, Katarki, Bruno, Hu, Ritchken, Jackson, Hu, Pancholi, Clancy, Colen, Wen, Leung, Xu, Padmanabha, He, Delimitrou, *ASPLOS 2019*) is the de facto standard benchmark: social network, media service, hotel reservation, e-commerce, banking, and "book info" workloads implemented as 20–40 microservices in multiple languages. **Sage** (Gan, Liang, Dev, Lo, Delimitrou, *ASPLOS 2021*) uses a causal Bayesian network plus graph neural networks to do root-cause analysis of QoS violations in microservice graphs, achieving 88% root-cause accuracy on DeathStar. **Sinan** (Zhang, Cheng, Delimitrou, *ASPLOS 2021*) uses a CNN to predict latency from resource allocations and selects allocations that minimize cost subject to SLO, with online learning to handle workload drift. These papers are the empirical backbone Monolift should evaluate against: a lifted DeathStar implementation, with and without runtime-directed placement, is the most persuasive possible demonstration.

Noisy-neighbor and co-location interference research (Delimitrou's Quasar, *ASPLOS 2014*; Paragon, *ASPLOS 2013*; Mars's Bubble-Up, *MICRO 2011*) established that compute-bound colocations can degrade each other by 2×–3× through LLC and memory-bandwidth contention. For Monolift, this is a hidden signal the delegate expression will see as a latency spike without an obvious cause.

### 4d. Serverless, FaaS, and the cold-start problem

A Monolift lift deployed as a Kubernetes pod is structurally a FaaS invocation. **SAND** (Akkus, Chen, Rimac-Drlje, Stein, Satzke, Beck, Aditya, Hilt, *ATC 2018*) introduced intra-application sandboxing and fast function-to-function communication via a message bus, cutting warm-start overhead to sub-millisecond. **Nightcore** (Jia and Witchel, *ASPLOS 2021*) co-locates microservice functions on a shared host runtime, achieving 1.36–2.93× throughput over containerized baselines with 69–85% lower tail latency. **Faasm** (Shillaker and Pietzuch, *ATC 2020*) uses WebAssembly "faaslets" with shared memory for fast communication; cold start drops to <10 ms. These systems are more than baselines — they are alternative backends Monolift could target if Kubernetes latency proves too coarse.

Cold-start numbers cluster by platform: AWS Lambda 100–500 ms for interpreted runtimes, 500–2000 ms for JVM, tens of ms for Rust/Go; Knative 2–5s for a fresh pod, sub-second for pre-warmed; Faasm sub-10 ms. The delegate expression threshold must be larger than the transition cost, which in the Kubernetes/Knative regime is at least hundreds of milliseconds. **This is the single strongest argument for pre-provisioning a "remote" replica before flipping any lift to remote**, and it is the feature Monolift probably most needs to add.

### 4e. Reinforcement learning for placement

**Pensieve** (Mao, Netravali, Alizadeh, *SIGCOMM 2017*) replaced hand-tuned adaptive-bitrate heuristics with an A3C-trained RL agent, improving QoE by 12–25%. It is the canonical *"an RL agent can learn a better threshold than you can tune by hand"* paper, and it is the closest intellectual template for replacing hand-written delegate expressions with a learned policy. **Decima** (Mao, Schwarzkopf, Venkatakrishnan, Meng, Alizadeh, *SIGCOMM 2019*) applies GNN+RL to DAG job scheduling and achieves 21% lower average JCT than Spark's default. **AuTO** (Chen, Lan, Chen, Zhang, Wu, Chen, *SIGCOMM 2018*) does coflow scheduling with DDPG. **Park** (Mao, Negi, Narayan, Wang, Yang, Wang, Marcus, Khani, He, Addanki, Venkatakrishnan, Sivaraman, Alizadeh, *NeurIPS 2019*) packages twelve systems problems as an RL benchmark and was explicit that "deploy RL in production systems" has four pitfalls: slow convergence, reward mis-specification, safety under exploration, and distribution shift.

For Monolift, the honest conclusion is that **RL-based placement is a 2027 thesis, not a 2026 workshop paper**. A learned policy that ignores a production constraint like "don't flap the payment service at checkout" can cost money. The more tractable near-term idea is *offline imitation learning* from a hand-tuned delegate expression, producing a differentiable policy that can be audited against the original rules.

### 4f. The oscillation and composability problem — the hardest open question

The single most important open problem in Monolift's current design is this: **if N delegate expressions are evaluated independently and each is locally stable, is the composition globally stable?** The answer in general is no, and the literature is clear about why.

The single-agent oscillation problem is well-understood. A lift transitions local→remote under load; the transition itself introduces latency (cold start, connection setup); the delegate expression sees the latency and flips back; the load is still there, so it flips again. Mitigations are well-known: **Schmitt trigger hysteresis** (two thresholds with a gap), **time-windowed averaging**, **exponential smoothing**, and **cooldown periods**. Kubernetes HPA implements all four; a recent arXiv preprint on Attention-Double-LSTM Kubernetes autoscaling (Aug 2025) quantifies the benefit of predictive smoothing, reporting replica churn drops from 716 to near zero when an LSTM filters stochastic noise. HeteroScale (Aug 2025, arXiv:2508.19559) explicitly enumerates anti-flapping as hysteresis thresholds plus cooldowns plus predictive dampening. Monolift's delegate expression language should expose these as primitives, not leave them to developers to re-derive.

The multi-agent problem is harder and is where the literature runs out. When delegate expressions on different lifts compete for shared resources — CPU on a node, bandwidth on a link, a downstream database's connection pool — their independent decisions constitute a **multi-agent control problem** with coupled dynamics. Classical results here come from game theory (Nash equilibria in congestion games, the price of anarchy, Roughgarden's *Selfish Routing*) and from multi-agent reinforcement learning (Lowe et al., *MADDPG, NeurIPS 2017*; Foerster et al., *COMA, AAAI 2018*). The depressing finding in MARL is that learned policies that are locally optimal can be jointly unstable, and that centralized critics are usually necessary for convergence. For Monolift, the practical options are:

1. **A central coordinator** that sees all delegate expressions and serializes transitions — simple, correct, non-scalable.
2. **Decentralized with a shared signal** — every delegate expression subscribes to a small set of global scalars (cluster load, tail latency, cost) and is constrained to react in bounded ways. This is how ECN works in TCP.
3. **Token-bucket admission for transitions** — a global rate limit on state changes per unit time, which bounds the worst-case oscillation frequency without requiring coordination.
4. **Learned joint policies** — MARL trained in a simulator of the lift graph, deployed with safety constraints.

**The Monolift paper does not currently pick one of these.** The cleanest workshop paper is a formal model of composed delegate expressions with a small (~3) number of explicitly categorized composition operators, each with a stability theorem.

## Section 5. Far memory, offloading, and the cost of distribution

The measurement literature gives the breakeven condition that any delegate expression must encode. The informal model is uniform across papers: a function with compute cost *C* (seconds), input/output sizes *D_in* and *D_out* (bytes), is worth executing remotely when *C − C'_remote > (D_in + D_out)/B + RTT + serialization*, where *B* is bandwidth, *RTT* round-trip time, and serialization includes marshaling and any coherence flush. MAUI's empirical finding — that offloading saves energy at 25 ms Wi-Fi RTT but *costs* energy at 220 ms 3G RTT — is the canonical instance. Across MAUI, CloneCloud, ThinkAir, and COMET the consistent message is that **only 30–60% of candidate methods are profitable to offload, and the profitable set shifts with network conditions.**

**Infiniswap** (Gu, Lee, Zhang, Chowdhury, Shin, *NSDI 2017*) is the page-granular anti-example: a kernel RDMA swap device delivered 4×–15× throughput gains on memory-bound workloads but exposes a brutal cost model — every heap pointer dereference is potentially a page fault. **LegoOS** (Shan, Huang, Chen, Zhang, *OSDI 2018*, Best Paper) pushed the model further with a splitkernel design but confirmed the same conclusion: transparent remote memory is only competitive when the unit of separation respects a natural API boundary. **AIFM** (Ruan, Schwarzkopf, Aguilera, Belay, *OSDI 2020*) changed the terms by exposing *application-integrated* semantics — remoteable pointers and containers over the Shenango μ-threaded runtime — and showed up to 61× wins over Fastswap. The AIFM thesis is Monolift's thesis: **application-semantic participation is what makes distribution efficient.** At compile time, Monolift knows what AIFM has to learn at runtime.

The cleanest breakeven measurement is Amaro, Branner-Augmon, Luo, Ousterhout, Aguilera, Panda, Ratnasamy, Shenker, *"Can Far Memory Improve Job Throughput?"* (*EuroSys 2020*). They build Fastswap and a far-memory-aware cluster scheduler CFM and answer empirically: far memory yields *single-digit to low-double-digit* throughput gains on memory-bound mixes and *zero or negative* gains on compute-bound workloads. Workload is characterized by two parameters, **m2c (memory-to-compute ratio) and packability**, and the scheduler optimizes savings in local-memory-time product per unit far-memory usage because the global problem is APX-hard. The lesson for Monolift is that the expected delegate-expression win on any individual lift is modest and that the real wins come from aggregate resource packing — which argues for a cluster-level coordinator rather than pure per-lift policy.

**TELEPORT** (Zhang, Chen, Sankhe, Zheng, Zhong, Angel, Chen, Liu, Loo, *SIGMOD 2022*) adds a first-class `pushdown(func, arg, flags)` system call to a splitkernel OS, with flags choosing eager/lazy synchronization and coherence relaxation. TELEPORT is Monolift at the OS layer: the flags are the delegate-expression semantics. **Offload Annotations** (Yuan, Palkar, Narayanan, Zaharia, *ATC 2020*) is the closest existing analog at the language layer: annotations on types and functions in a CPU library (NumPy, Pandas) that map them to equivalent GPU-library implementations, with the Bach runtime dispatching per-call based on estimated speedup minus transfer cost. Reported speedups of up to 1200× with median 6.3× on NumPy/Pandas pipelines. The OA pattern — **static annotations + runtime dispatch + per-call cost model** — is exactly Monolift's pattern, and the Monolift paper should cite OA as the direct antecedent.

**CXL** changes the physics of the breakeven inequality by dropping interconnect RTT from tens of microseconds (RDMA) to hundreds of nanoseconds. CXL 2.0 adds switching and memory pooling, CXL 3.0 and 3.1 add multi-level fabrics and rack-scale coherency. Pond (Li et al., *ASPLOS 2023*) shows real benefits in reclaiming *stranded* memory in cloud platforms; the HotNets 2023 paper "A Case Against CXL Memory Pooling" argues pooling gains are overstated at typical VM granularities. For Monolift the implication is that many lifts that are unprofitable on RDMA become profitable on CXL-attached memory — and that the cost model in the delegate expression should be **parametric in the interconnect tier**.

**Hermit** (Qiao et al., *NSDI 2023*) and **Midas** (Qiao et al., *NSDI 2024*) extend the AIFM lineage with feedback-directed async and application-managed soft state; both confirm that cost-model accuracy comes from online measurement rather than static estimates. The **Quicksand/Nu** line (Ruan et al., *NSDI 2023, 2025*) goes further with microsecond-scale logical-process migration — effectively the runtime Monolift's compiler would target if it ever dropped the "no migration" bound.

## Section 6. Multitier and tierless programming

Weisenburger, Wirth, and Salvaneschi's *Survey of Multitier Programming* (*ACM Computing Surveys 2020*) is the right map. It taxonomizes systems along axes of **placement strategy** (explicit annotation, inference, mixed), **communication style** (remote call, reactive signal, channel), and **consistency model** (none, eventual, transactional). Against this taxonomy, Monolift is *explicit annotation* + *remote call* + *no shared consistency* — a deliberately minimalist point in the space.

**Links** (Cooper, Lindley, Wadler, Yallop, *FMCO 2006*) is the founding example: a single functional language for three tiers, with location annotations and type-directed call-site transformation. **ScalaLoci** (Weisenburger, Köhler, Salvaneschi, *OOPSLA 2018*) adds first-class placement types to Scala and supports reactive propagation across peers. **Hop.js** (Serrano et al.) and **Ur/Web** (Chlipala, *ICFP 2015*) attack the web-tier variant with type-safe server–client split. **Eliom** (Balat et al., OCaml) and **Volta** (Microsoft Research) complete the lineage.

The uniform weakness of the multitier literature is that every system **requires upfront adoption**. You write Links; you port to ScalaLoci; you commit to Ur/Web. This is the adoption cost Monolift explicitly rejects. **Monolift's genuine novelty against multitier is legacy-compatibility**: it adds lifts to unmodified Go rather than requiring a new language. A defensible Monolift framing is "a multitier system whose source language is an existing industrial language and whose placement is runtime-directed." On the Weisenburger axes, this is a previously unoccupied cell.

## Section 7. Choreographic programming

Choreographies describe a protocol from a global viewpoint; *endpoint projection* derives the per-participant implementation mechanically, and a deadlock-freedom theorem relates the two. The lineage is Carbone, Honda, and Yoshida's multiparty session types (*POPL 2008, JACM 2016*), Montesi's **Chor** (*PhD thesis 2013*), and the object-oriented **Choral** (Giallorenzo, Montesi, Peressotti, *TOPLAS 2024*; earlier ECOOP 2020 version). **Pirouette** (Hirsch and Garg, *POPL 2023*) is the functional formalism, giving a dependently-typed λ-calculus for choreographies with a fully formalized endpoint projection and a proof that the projected processes simulate the choreography. **Klor** is the Clojure embedding. Giallorenzo, Montesi, Peressotti's *ECOOP 2021* bridge paper shows that multitier languages and choreographies are two descriptions of the same object up to endpoint projection.

The question this literature asks of Monolift is **can the Monolift compiler pass be characterized as endpoint projection from an implicit choreography?** If yes, Monolift inherits a correctness theorem for free: the monolith and the lifted deployment are observationally equivalent modulo the lift protocol. If no, Monolift owes its own correctness argument. The answer is probably "yes, for the subset of lifts whose state is isolated, and no, for lifts that share state through channels or shared data structures." A formal workshop paper casting Monolift's IR as a choreography calculus with projection into Go and RPC is one of the cleanest theoretical contributions the team could make.

## Section 8. State, concurrency, and distributed correctness

Monolift's bounded model — no global state, no heap sharing, no migration — is the classical distributed-systems tax, and every mechanism in this section is an approach to (a) ruling out problematic sharing statically, (b) making it deterministic at runtime, or (c) restoring correctness through protocol.

**Rust's ownership and borrowing** discipline enforces that every value has either one owner with mutation rights or multiple shared readers. A moved value can be deep-copied across a wire safely; a shared borrow can be replicated as a read-only snapshot. Go has no such discipline, so Monolift must reconstruct it via escape analysis: refuse any lift whose arguments are mutable aliased references or whose closures capture shared mutable state. This is the single biggest static-analysis obligation Monolift inherits. **Pony's deny capabilities** (Clebsch et al., *AGERE! 2015*) generalize the Rust idea with a richer lattice; they are the formal template for "sendable across a lift boundary."

**LVars** (Kuper and Newton, *FHPC 2013*; Kuper, Turon, Krishnaswami, Newton, *POPL 2014*) give an escape hatch. An LVar is a shared location whose state moves monotonically up a lattice; reads are threshold reads that block until a bound is reached. Monolithic patterns that look like shared state — counters, sets, caches with union semantics — are often LVars in disguise, and Monolift should recognize them as safe to distribute. **CRDTs** (Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero, Zawirski, *SSS 2011*) are the distributed analog: replicas converge via lattice join or commutative operations. The researcher preference is to de-emphasize CRDTs, which is correct: CRDTs are the right tool when the shared state is genuinely mergeable, but most monolith shared state is not.

**The CALM theorem** (Hellerstein and Alvaro, *CACM 2020*, "Keeping CALM: When Distributed Consistency is Easy") is the theoretical hammer. A program has a coordination-free distributed implementation **if and only if** it is expressible in monotonic logic. This partitions the lift-safety question cleanly: lift exactly the monotone sub-graphs, refuse to lift non-monotone ones unless the programmer supplies an explicit coordination escape. Monolift's correctness story is essentially this: **monotone lifts are free, non-monotone lifts are opt-in and more expensive.** The CALM framing gives a principled characterization of when the "no shared state" restriction is actually the right restriction.

**Session types** (Honda, Vasconcelos, Kubo, *ESOP 1998*; Honda, Yoshida, Carbone, *POPL 2008, JACM 2016*) type communication protocols. For Monolift, every lift's RPC edge has an implicit session type — the pre/post conditions of the call — and multiparty session types (MPST) are the apparatus for checking that bi-directional streams and transactional sequences stay in protocol. **Gabet and Yoshida's session-type-inspired Go analysis** (*ECOOP 2020*) is particularly relevant because it handles Go's channels and mutexes directly; Monolift can reuse this analysis to validate its generated RPC stubs.

The escape-hatch ladder for non-monotone lifts is well-established: **2PC** (Gray 1978) for atomicity at the cost of availability; **sagas** (Garcia-Molina and Salem, *SIGMOD 1987*) for long-running transactions with programmer-supplied compensations; **durable execution** via **Temporal** or **Cadence** for checkpointed workflow state with automatic retry/replay on failure. Each rung is more expressive but more expensive. Monolift's compiler's job is to pick the cheapest rung that satisfies the program's invariants and to make the choice visible in the annotation surface.

Common monolith anti-patterns that violate "no shared state," empirically:

| Pattern | Problem on lift | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| In-memory caches (`sync.Map`, custom LRU) | Cache shared across handlers | Distributed cache (Redis) or recognize as LVar |
| Process-local locks (`sync.Mutex`) | No shared address space for the lock | Rework as idempotent/monotone |
| `context.Context` with non-serializable values | Request-scoped pointers to DB handles | Re-inject equivalents on remote side |
| Goroutine closures capturing heap pointers | Captures dangle across processes | Materialize as explicit serializable args |
| Package-level `init()` state | Not replicated to lifted process | Pin to one host or deterministic init |
| `chan T` shared across lifted code | Channels are in-process | Generate queue/stream; check via MPST |
| `time.Now()`, `rand.Rand` | Non-deterministic across hosts | Seeded RNG or tag as non-replay-safe |

Each pattern is a compiler decision: reject, rewrite, or escalate.

## Section 9. Compiler infrastructure and IRs

**MLIR** (Lattner, Amini, Bondhugula, Cohen, Davis, Pienaar, Riddle, Shpeisman, Vasilache, Zinenko, *CGO 2021*) is multi-level IR with user-defined dialects. A Monolift dialect is a plausible direction: the lift annotation becomes an MLIR op with attributes for the delegate expression and deployment target, and lowering passes produce either in-process Go or gRPC-plus-Kubernetes manifests. This would open the door to non-Go source languages sharing the same Monolift backend. The cost is reimplementing the Go SSA frontend against MLIR, which is not trivial.

**GraalVM and Truffle** provide language-agnostic compilation and partial evaluation across multiple source languages; they are the closest thing to a production language-portable substrate. **LLVM** is the universal backend and is the right target for AOT-compiled Go under TinyGo. **WebAssembly** — and specifically the 2024–2025 **Component Model** (WASI Preview 3 trajectory, Wasmtime 24+) — is the most plausible long-term distribution substrate: a lift compiled to a WASM component is platform-agnostic, fast to start (sub-10 ms in Faasm-class runtimes), and composable across language boundaries. Cloudflare Workers and Fastly Compute@Edge have production deployments of this model.

On program analysis, **Soot** and **WALA** are the JVM-side tools; **CodeQL** and **Infer** (Meta) add query-based and abstract-interpretation-based analyses. On the Go side, the story is thinner but sufficient: `golang.org/x/tools/go/ssa` produces an SSA form suitable for dataflow; `go/analysis` is the standard framework for pluggable analyzers; `gopls` exposes the same analyses to editors. The key gaps for Monolift are (i) a production-quality escape analysis beyond what the Go runtime does internally, and (ii) a points-to analysis robust to Go's interface and reflection. **GopherJS** and **TinyGo** compile Go to JS and WASM respectively; TinyGo in particular is a realistic target for a WASM-backed Monolift variant.

## Section 10. Benchmarking and evaluation methodology

The microservice benchmarking question for Monolift is: **how do you fairly compare a lifted monolith against a hand-written microservice system?** DeathStarBench (Gan et al., *ASPLOS 2019*) is the unavoidable starting point: it provides six end-to-end applications with 20–40 services each, including social network, hotel reservation, and media serving. The gaps DeathStarBench does not fill are (a) workloads explicitly designed to stress placement decisions (bursty, skewed, multi-tenant) and (b) workloads where the monolith-versus-microservice question has a known answer. **μSuite** (Sriraman and Wenisch) and **TailBench** (Kasture and Sanchez, *IISWC 2016*) complete the microservice-benchmark landscape.

**Statistical rigor** is the other half of the evaluation. Georges, Buytaert, Eeckhout's *Statistically Rigorous Java Performance Evaluation* (*OOPSLA 2007*) and Kalibera and Jones's *Rigorous Benchmarking in Reasonable Time* (*ISMM 2013*) are the standard references. The relevant metrics for Monolift are p50 and p99 latency, throughput, cost per request (cores × $/core-hour), annotation burden (annotations per KLOC), debuggability (mean time to root cause on a synthetic fault injection), and — uniquely — **the number of placement decisions per second and the oscillation frequency**, which conventional microservice benchmarks do not measure.

A defensible evaluation plan: (i) take three DeathStarBench applications, (ii) implement each as pure Go monolith + Monolift annotations + hand-written microservices + Service Weaver, (iii) measure all five systems on identical hardware with identical load profiles including bursts and fan-out skew, (iv) report the full latency CDFs with confidence intervals via bootstrap, and (v) report the annotation count and the delegate-expression transition frequency. This is the evaluation that would make Monolift's claims stick.

## Section 11. Modern cloud-native and serverless frameworks

The competitive landscape is crowded and converging. The table below compares seven systems on the five dimensions that matter for Monolift.

| System | Legacy code? | Annotation burden | Dynamic placement | State model | Maturity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Service Weaver | No (requires component model) | Medium (`Implements[T]`, `Ref[T]`) | Config, not live | In-memory + explicit state | Beta, Google-backed |
| Encore.dev | No (new code) | Medium (decorators, types) | No | Typed databases | Commercial, production |
| Winglang/Wing | No (new language) | High (preflight/inflight) | No | Platform resources | Early |
| Dapr | Yes (sidecar, any lang) | Low (HTTP/gRPC calls) | No | State stores, actors | Production |
| Temporal/Cadence | Partial (workflows are new) | High (workflow code) | No | Durable event log | Production |
| Knative | Yes (any container) | Low (no code change) | Scale-to-zero | Stateless recommended | Production |
| **Monolift** | **Yes (comment annotations)** | **Low to medium** | **Yes (delegate expressions)** | **Bounded, no shared** | **Research** |

Monolift is the only entry with "yes" in both the legacy column and the dynamic-placement column. Service Weaver is the closest in legacy-tolerance within Go but is static; Knative is legacy-tolerant but operates on containers, not call sites; Dapr is runtime-side but does not change application code. **The distinctive combination for Monolift is legacy-plus-dynamic, and this is where the paper should plant its flag.**

**Ballerina** and **Nitric** and **Hatchet/Inngest** are further away: Ballerina is a new language for integration, Nitric is infrastructure-from-code, Hatchet and Inngest are event-driven workflow runtimes. They are alternatives to parts of the Monolift stack rather than competitors to its core.

## Section 12. Developer experience, annotations, and debugging

The annotation-ergonomics lineage is anchored by **OpenMP** (`#pragma omp parallel for`, 1997–), which proved that pragma-based parallelism can be adopted incrementally by existing C/C++ codebases, and **OpenMP 4.x `#pragma omp target`** (2013–), which extended the model to GPU and accelerator offloading. **OpenACC** (2011–) is the parallel lineage on the Nvidia side, and **CUDA's `__device__`/`__global__`** qualifiers are the explicit-partition variant. The analogy to Monolift's `//monolift:offload` is direct. The empirical lesson from two decades of OpenMP is that **pragmas are adopted when they are additive and degrade gracefully**: an `#pragma` that is ignored by a non-OpenMP compiler must still leave a correct sequential program. Monolift satisfies this property by construction.

**Debugging a program that sometimes runs locally and sometimes remotely** is an under-solved problem. Distributed tracing (Jaeger, Zipkin, OpenTelemetry) is the production answer: every call boundary emits spans with causal parent-child links, and a trace UI reconstructs the request flow. The cost is per-call overhead (typically 1–5% of latency) and the requirement that every service participate. **Time-travel debugging** (Mozilla's `rr` for Linux/x86, Microsoft's WinDbg TTD) works beautifully for single-process executions and does not compose across processes; extending it to distributed executions is an open research area (Chothia et al., *ConcDB*). For Monolift specifically, there is a novel opportunity: because the compiler knows which lifts are live-remote and which are local in the current run, a debugger could present a unified call stack that elides the RPC boundary when the developer wants the "logical" view and exposes it when they want the "physical" view. This is a UX paper waiting to be written.

The mental-model literature (Ko, LaToza, Myers; Begel and Zimmermann on cognitive load in codebases) suggests that developers reason about distributed programs in one of two modes: *message-passing* (where they see channels and RPCs) or *call-graph* (where they see functions). Monolift's annotation surface deliberately preserves the call-graph mode while adding a tiny message-passing sub-language (the delegate expression). This is a defensible choice but it trades the ability to reason about message ordering — Waldo's concurrency issue — for simplicity.

**LLM-guided annotation synthesis (2023–2025)** is the hot area that intersects most directly with Monolift's plausible automation story. The MonoEmbed line (arXiv:2502.04604, Feb 2025) uses LLM embeddings plus contrastive learning to propose microservice decompositions. Trabelsi, Moha, Guéhéneuc (*ICSOC 2024* workshop, 2026 proceedings) explicitly argue for LLM-assisted migration with systematic prompt templates. The 2025 *Information and Software Technology* mapping study cites ten such systems. **None of them produces Monolift-style annotations**, which is an opportunity: an LLM fine-tuned to suggest lift annotations plus delegate expressions on an unmodified Go codebase is a plausible, cleanly-scoped 2026 paper.

## Section 13. Security and trust at lift boundaries

When a lift becomes remote, it crosses a trust boundary. **Swift** (Chong, Liu, Myers, Qi, Vikram, Zheng, Zheng, *SOSP 2007*) and **Jif** (Myers and Liskov, *TOCS 2000*) use information-flow labels to drive partitioning so that secrets never cross an untrusted channel; **Fabric** (Liu, George, Vikram, Qi, Waye, Myers, *SOSP 2009*) extends the discipline to distributed storage with explicit declassification. These systems give the formal apparatus Monolift would need to claim security properties at lift boundaries — but as noted earlier, their annotation cost is Jif-class, which is prohibitive for legacy code.

In Kubernetes, the practical tooling already exists. **mTLS** at the service mesh layer (Istio, Linkerd) encrypts all service-to-service traffic. **SPIFFE/SPIRE** gives cryptographic workload identity, so a lift dispatched as a pod can authenticate itself to its caller with a signed SVID rather than a shared secret. **NetworkPolicy** resources constrain which pods can reach which. Monolift should generate SPIFFE identities and NetworkPolicy rules as part of its Kubernetes backend, making mTLS and least-privilege networking the default rather than a configuration afterthought.

**Side channels** at lift boundaries are a real and underexamined risk. Timing channels — where the duration of a remote call leaks information about inputs — are amplified by distribution because the attacker can measure from the network. Traffic-analysis attacks on service meshes (Rimmer et al., 2022; various CCS papers on encrypted RPC fingerprinting) have shown that even mTLS traffic reveals call structure through packet timing and size. The defenses are padding, constant-time implementations, and delay shaping, none of which compose naturally with delegate expressions that *optimize* for latency. There is a genuine tension between Monolift's performance-driven placement and a timing-side-channel-resistant deployment, and it should be acknowledged in the threat model.

**Zero-trust networking** (BeyondCorp, SPIFFE-based architectures) shifts the trust model from network-boundary to per-request authentication. Monolift's dual dispatch fits this cleanly: a local call has process-level trust; a remote call gets per-call SPIFFE-backed authN/Z; the delegate expression can be extended to account for the authN cost when deciding to flip.

## Section 14. Platform generalization

The Kubernetes assumption is the biggest portability question. **AWS Lambda** has lower cold-start latency for simple runtimes (sub-100 ms for Rust/Go) but imposes strict execution-time and size limits. **Google Cloud Run** and **Azure Container Apps** are container-native serverless and are the most plausible non-Kubernetes targets for Monolift; both use Knative Serving internally, so a Knative-based Monolift backend would be portable across all three. **Knative** itself is a Kubernetes-native serverless layer with scale-to-zero and request-based autoscaling, and is the natural near-term extension to Monolift's backend.

The more radical platform generalization is the **WebAssembly Component Model**. A lift compiled to a WASM component is small (tens to hundreds of KB vs. hundreds of MB for a container image), fast to start (single-digit ms vs. hundreds of ms), and platform-agnostic (runs on Wasmtime, WasmEdge, browsers, edge compute). TinyGo already compiles a useful subset of Go to WASM. The research question is whether Monolift's lift IR is rich enough to lower to a WASM component, which in turn requires solving the async/goroutine-to-WASM mapping — an active area in the WASI Preview 3 proposal (late 2024 / 2025). A WASM-backed Monolift would also naturally extend to **edge and multi-cloud** deployments, where the same lift could run on a Cloudflare Worker, a Fastly edge, or an in-cluster pod depending on the delegate expression's signal.

## Section 15. The unknown unknowns

Five adjacent areas deserve mention because they are likely to matter for Monolift's next five years and are underrepresented in the brief.

**Unikernels and library OSes.** MirageOS (Madhavapeddy, Mortier, Rotsos, Scott, Singh, Gazagnaire, Smith, Hand, Crowcroft, *ASPLOS 2013*) and Unikraft (Kuenzer et al., *EuroSys 2021*) compile an application plus just the OS functionality it needs into a single unikernel image; startup latencies drop to tens of milliseconds and image sizes to single-digit MB. If a Monolift lift were deployed as a Unikraft instance rather than a Kubernetes pod, cold-start cost would drop by an order of magnitude and the delegate-expression transition threshold could be correspondingly lower. This is a direct engineering win that does not require any theoretical extension.

**eBPF observability.** Cilium, Pixie, Parca, and the Tetragon lineage instrument kernel syscalls and network traffic in the Linux kernel without any application-side change. The opportunity for Monolift is that delegate expressions need performance signals — latency, contention, saturation — and eBPF can produce them without instrumenting the Go runtime. An eBPF-based Monolift observability layer is a low-risk and high-leverage contribution.

**Confidential computing.** Intel SGX, AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX, and ARM CCA provide hardware-enforced memory encryption and attestation. Lift boundaries can be promoted to confidentiality boundaries by running a lifted function in an enclave; the attestation token becomes part of the delegate expression's trust evaluation. This is a paper's worth of work and is particularly relevant for multi-tenant or edge deployments.

**Formal verification.** IronFleet (Hawblitzel et al., *SOSP 2015*), Verdi (Wilcox, Woos, Panchekha, Tatlock, Wang, Ernst, Anderson, *PLDI 2015*), and Ivy (Padon, McMillan, Panda, Sagiv, Shoham, *PLDI 2016*) verify distributed-systems implementations against temporal-logic specifications. If Monolift's compiler pass is cast as endpoint projection (Section 7), the correctness obligation reduces to "the generated RPC matches the local call's contract," which is small enough to be proof-tractable for a bounded lift. This is a multi-year project but it is the clearest theoretical payoff.

**Edge computing and federated learning.** At the edge, resources are heterogeneous, connectivity is intermittent, and data is sensitive. Monolift's bounded lift model — no shared state, no migration — happens to be exactly the shape that works at the edge, because it does not require coherent global state. The research question is whether delegate expressions can express edge-aware placement (e.g., "run this lift on the nearest edge node with ≥2 GB free and a working uplink"). **Differential privacy** becomes relevant when lifted code handles sensitive data and gets placed on untrusted infrastructure; annotating lifts with privacy budgets and enforcing them at the compiler is another plausible direction.

Two more brief mentions: **program synthesis** (Solar-Lezama's Sketch; Rosette) could frame the annotation-suggestion problem as synthesis from cost and correctness constraints, complementing LLM-based suggestion with verified solutions; and **persistent memory plus CXL 3.0** change the far-memory cost model (Section 5) enough that a delegate expression parameterized by the interconnect tier becomes not just reasonable but necessary.

## Section 16. A consolidated research agenda

Three time horizons organize the work.

**Near-term (workshop-scale, 6–9 months).** First: a head-to-head evaluation of Monolift, Service Weaver, a hand-written microservice baseline, and Knative on three DeathStarBench workloads, measuring p50/p99/cost/annotation-count/oscillation-frequency — this is the evaluation that makes the paper's claims defensible and directly addresses the Service Weaver comparison that reviewers will demand. Second: a formal characterization of delegate-expression composition with three named operators (independent, shared-signal, serialized) and a stability theorem for each — the cleanest response to Section 4f's open question. Third: an eBPF-based signal layer that feeds delegate expressions without Go-runtime instrumentation, building on Cilium/Pixie primitives.

**Medium-term (conference-scale, 12–18 months).** First: a Monolift variant that compiles lifts to WebAssembly Components and deploys to a mix of Knative, Cloudflare Workers, and in-cluster pods, with the delegate expression choosing across all three based on latency and cost — this is the "beyond Go + Kubernetes" fault line turned into a paper. Second: an LLM-based annotation suggester fine-tuned to propose lift plus delegate-expression sets on unannotated Go codebases, with a user study of acceptance rate and correctness — this lands in ICSE/FSE territory. Third: a CALM-based static analysis that categorizes each candidate lift as monotone-safe, monotone-with-LVar, or non-monotone-requires-coordination, and picks the cheapest coordination rung (2PC / saga / Temporal) for the third category.

**Long-term (thesis-scale, 24–36 months).** First: a learned multi-agent placement policy that replaces hand-tuned delegate expressions, trained in a Park-style simulator of the lift graph and deployed with safety constraints — this is the Decima-to-Monolift analog. Second: a formally-verified Monolift pass, cast as endpoint projection in a Pirouette-class calculus, with a mechanized proof of observational equivalence between the monolith and the lifted deployment — this is the theoretical capstone. Third: a confidential-computing extension in which lift boundaries are trust boundaries with attestation-in-the-delegate-expression, targeting edge and multi-tenant deployments.

The common thread across all three horizons is that **the transition function is the problem**. Every direction in the near-term builds signals or primitives that a better transition function can consume; every direction in the medium-term relaxes a constraint on where the transition can happen; and every direction in the long-term is some form of making the transition function itself smarter or verified.
